# Text alongside visuals

Use this guidance when shaping explanatory text for slides, diagrams, charts, or visual summaries. Start with what the audience needs to understand, remember, or decide. Interface actions, form labels, validation errors, and loading states need interface-specific guidance.

## Let text and visuals work together

Give each slide or visual section a clear purpose. A title can state the supported takeaway rather than merely name the topic. “Most requests finish within two seconds” is more informative than “Performance” when the data supports that claim. A neutral topic heading remains appropriate for an agenda or reference view.

Use captions to explain significance, conditions, or unfamiliar relationships. Remove text that only repeats a heading, visible label, or obvious visual feature. Keep units, time periods, comparison baselines, and sources needed to interpret the evidence. An accessible text description may deliberately repeat visual information for readers who cannot see it.

Keep names consistent between prose, labels, and legends. Place an explanation close to what it qualifies. Explain an unfamiliar abbreviation before expecting the audience to interpret it.

## Shorten without losing the claim's limits

Prefer direct phrases and remove filler before removing evidence. Do not turn an observed association into a causal claim or a proposal into an achieved result. For example, shortening “In a pilot with 20 participants, median setup time fell from 10 to 7 minutes” to “Setup is 30% faster” loses the population, measure, and limits of the observation.

Put secondary detail in notes or supporting material when the audience can access it. Keep qualifications that change the meaning beside the claim. Slides circulated without a presenter need enough context to stand alone; speaker notes cannot substitute for context readers never receive.

Repeat orientation or consequential caveats when a slide may be read independently. Avoid an absolute rule that an explanation may appear only once.

## Review in the intended form

Check whether the text remains legible and understandable at the intended viewing size. Look for wrapping, truncation, crowded labels, and meanings conveyed only through color or position. Use the presentation or design workflow to inspect a render when available; do not claim visual verification from text alone.

Choose length for the audience and layout rather than a fixed word count. A separate editorial skill or readability score is not required.
